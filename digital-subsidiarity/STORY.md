# Digital Subsidiarity

*Infrastructure designed not merely to give communities sovereignty, but to teach them how to keep it.*

## Prologue
<!-- viz: mesh | year: 0 | era: The Trusted Community | status: Founding | accent: #e0b25c -->

On today's Internet you are a *user*. Someone else owns the machinery, writes the terms, stores your memory, and decides what can reach you. When something breaks, you appeal upward to a company whose inner workings you will never see.

A different design is possible: families, parishes, schools, and cooperatives that **own and govern their own digital life**, their communications, data, computation, and relationships, instead of renting access to them.

But ownership is only the beginning. Over a long enough horizon, one question decides everything.

>> Who will be capable of stewarding such a system after its builders are gone?

## I. The Initial Vision
<!-- viz: roll | year: 1 | era: Foundations | status: Built | accent: #e0b25c -->

*Concept v0.1* supplies the essential insight: **subsidiarity can be made concrete** as a network security and governance model, built from five primitives: Identity, Roll, Transport, Content, and Ledger.

Its first move is to treat the **trusted community as a first-class technical object.** The *Roll* is not merely an access-control list. It joins membership, authorization, discovery, obligation, governance, hosting, and federation into a social object that machines can enforce.

Its second move reverses the Internet's default. Instead of universal reachability followed by endless filtering, it proposes **relationship before reachability.** A path exists because a person or community chose to establish one.

And it understands that **ownership matters.** Whoever owns the infrastructure on which memory, education, and intelligence depend possesses consequential power.

> Those are strong foundations. The augmentation begins where their greatest long-term vulnerability appears.

## II. The Fifty-Year Test
<!-- viz: fade | year: 5 | until: 50 | era: The Long Test | status: Drifting | accent: #d79a52 -->

Imagine a parish deploys the system. A gifted technical leader configures its networking, identity, mail, backups, and gateway. The pastor supports it. Parishioners use it. For several years it works beautifully.

Now return twenty years later. The founder has moved away. A second administrator has died. Pastors have come and gone. Hardware has been replaced, dependencies have shifted, and the appliance's manufacturer no longer exists.

The parish secretary needs a sacramental record *now*, and nobody present remembers why the system was configured the way it was.

If it fails under those conditions, it was never sovereign. It merely exchanged dependence on a large company for dependence on **one generation of gifted people.**

>> Authority succession is not the same as competence succession.

A Roll can prove, cryptographically, that new stewards hold legitimate authority. It cannot make them capable. A system built on subsidiarity must be designed not only to distribute authority but to **reproduce competence.**

## III. The Ecclesial Parallel
<!-- viz: communion | year: 50 | era: Communion | status: Federated | accent: #93b7d4 -->

The Church is both local and universal. It does not scale by making one institution larger. It scales through **communion, formation, office, tradition, federation, and succession.**

A parish is genuinely local, yet it need not reproduce every competence of the universal Church. A diocese does what exceeds a parish. Authority lives at many levels without erasing the identity of the lowest.

>> Subsidiarity does not mean everything must be done locally. It means responsibility should not be removed from the lowest *competent* level unnecessarily.

That word, *competent*, matters. Competence has to be cultivated. The Church teaches before it commissions. It apprentices. It creates offices that precede their occupants and survive them.

Digital subsidiarity requires an analogous culture.

## IV. Stewardship as Vocation
<!-- viz: steward | year: 52 | era: Vocation | status: Received | accent: #e8b862 -->

Consumer technology teaches an anthropology through repetition. The user chooses; the provider operates. The user accepts terms; the provider changes them. Over years, this trains people to experience digital life as **consumption.**

A subsidiarity network should form the opposite disposition: **stewardship.** A steward receives something that matters, cares for it, exercises bounded authority over it, stays accountable, teaches others, and eventually hands it on.

So stewardship is not just an administrator permission in a schema. It is a **vocation within the community.**

A twelve-year-old learns to tend the family archive. A student helps run a classroom service. A volunteer apprentices under an experienced steward. A professional serves a regional cooperative. Eventually, each one trains a successor.

>> The community does not merely possess sovereign technology. It produces people capable of sovereignty.

## V. Formation Is the Scaling Strategy
<!-- viz: bloom | year: 55 | era: Scale | status: Growing | accent: #9ccf8f -->

This changes what scale means. The platform model is: acquire users, centralize operations, increase efficiency, grow the platform.

The model here is closer to: **form stewards, establish a community, develop local competence, federate where useful, form more stewards.**

The unit of growth is not the user. It is **the competent steward and the competent community.**

A million passive users dependent on a small engineering company would be failure by the project's own principles, even if the software were decentralized. Ten thousand communities able to govern, repair, migrate, and hand on their systems would be genuine scale.

>> We scale when communities reproduce stewardship faster than they reproduce dependency.

## VI. A Sixth Primitive
<!-- viz: primitives | year: 58 | era: The Sixth Primitive | status: Defined | accent: #e0b25c -->

*Concept v0.1* names five primitives: Identity, Roll, Transport, Content, Ledger. The augmented vision proposes a sixth, **Stewardship**: the machinery by which responsibility survives changes in personnel and generations.

::: stats
Formation | how a member learns enough to take on more responsibility
Apprenticeship | how practical knowledge moves from steward to steward
Office | duties that attach to a role, not to whoever holds it
Succession | authority and competence, transferred on purpose
Audit | how a community knows its systems actually work
Recovery | proving, not assuming, that it can rebuild from loss
Documentation | memory usable by the next steward
Migration | new hardware or vendors without losing identity
Interchangeability | replacing a steward without collapse
Continuing formation | staying competent as threats change
:::

Some of these live in software. Others are curricula, offices, ceremonies, standards, certification, and apprenticeship. That is not a defect: **the social architecture and the technical architecture must be designed together.**

## VII. Leadership Without Lordship
<!-- viz: keystone | year: 62 | era: Accountability | status: Auditable | accent: #e0a24a -->

Decentralization alone does not remove concentrations of power. Suppose the parish formally owns its server, but only one parishioner understands how it runs. That person holds real authority, whatever the governance document says.

>> The danger is replacing "Google controls our digital life" with "the one person who knows Linux controls our digital life."

Expertise should carry authority, but it must stay **serviceable, accountable, auditable, and transferable.** A mature installation makes its steward powerful enough to maintain it, and structurally replaceable.

**Boring technology is often virtuous.** Every novelty spends some of a community's finite capacity to understand its own system.

**Exit must be designed before entry.** **Recovery must be practiced:** a backup never restored is an assertion, not resilience.

And **complexity has a moral cost.** It transfers power toward specialists. So the question for every feature is not only *can we build it?* but:

>> Can this community responsibly inherit it?

## VIII. Subsidiary Institutions
<!-- viz: ecosystem | year: 65 | era: Interdependence | status: Supported | accent: #8fc9c4 -->

Local ownership does not require local self-sufficiency. Demanding that every parish employ an expert in cryptography, networking, mail delivery, storage, inference, and security would itself violate subsidiarity, assigning work to a level not competent to do it.

The healthier model is an ecosystem. A parish owns its identity, records, policies, and hardware, while a **regional cooperative** maintains them. A **diocese** offers disaster recovery and training without routine visibility into parish communications.

**Independent providers** maintain compatible installations, so replacing one does not mean replacing your identity. A **trade association** trains and certifies stewards without owning the networks they serve.

>> The objective is not isolation. It is interdependence without unnecessary domination.

## IX. The Educational Program
<!-- viz: ladder | year: 70 | era: Formation | status: Taught | accent: #93b7d4 -->

If stewardship is the central vocation, education cannot be an afterthought. There should be a deliberate **ladder of digital formation.**

At the first rung, members learn that files, messages, identities, and networks are **real things cared for by real people,** not invisible magic supplied by "the cloud."

Students learn why permissions exist, why backups matter, what encryption protects, and what metadata reveals. Older students learn identity, networking, storage, and recovery through responsibility for actual bounded systems. Adults apprentice into operational roles.

The highest rung is not technical mastery. It is **judgment.**

>> What should be local? What should be shared? What should never be collected? When should authority be delegated upward, and when should it return? What must I teach my successor before I leave?

## X. Reframing the School
<!-- viz: school | year: 72 | era: The School | status: Learning | accent: #9ccf8f -->

The original draft imagines a school without Google Classroom. That remains a compelling demonstration, but the measure of success changes.

The goal is not merely *"students used locally owned infrastructure for a year."* It is that **students graduated understanding that digital systems are things human beings can understand, govern, maintain, and take responsibility for.**

A school could teach *through* its infrastructure. Older students help maintain bounded parts of the system, inspect the policies under which their data is held, join backup exercises, and learn why the school drew its boundaries where it did.

>> Not merely privacy from a technology company, but formation in digital agency. The student ceases to be only a user. The student becomes a steward.

## XI. A Different Kind of Company
<!-- viz: exit | year: 75 | era: A Different Company | status: Portable | accent: #e0b25c -->

This changes the business model. Ordinary SaaS economics rewards making cancellation difficult and centralizing expertise inside the vendor. A company committed to subsidiarity should **deliberately create customers capable of replacing it.**

That sounds paradoxical, but durable professions already work this way. Electricians, accountants, architects, and educators create value without owning their clients.

It can sell reliable appliances, maintenance, training, certification, migrations, security updates, audits, disaster recovery, and specialized compute, while the community's identity and essential records **remain portable.**

>> You own this. We will teach you how to steward it. You can replace us without losing it.

## XII. The Philosopher-Builder Test
<!-- viz: compass | year: 80 | era: The Test | status: Examined | accent: #b9a6e0 -->

Technical mechanisms embody philosophical choices. For every important feature, ask:

::: stats
The person | what conception of the person does it assume?
The level | where should responsibility for it reside?
Competence | what is required to exercise that responsibility?
Teaching | how will that competence be taught?
Succession | how will responsibility pass to the next steward?
Replaceability | can the community inspect and replace those who serve it?
:::

>> Will this feature make future members more capable stewards, or more dependent consumers?

A technically excellent feature that produces helpless users may be inconsistent with the project. A slightly less efficient one that keeps responsibility intelligible and transferable may be better.

Efficiency is not the highest good of the system. **Human and institutional maturity is.**

## XIII. One Sentence
<!-- viz: field | year: 85 | era: The Thesis | status: Stated | accent: #e6c877 -->

>>> A network of communities that own and govern their digital life at the lowest competent level, and deliberately form each generation to inherit, steward, and hand on that responsibility.

Or, more compactly:

>>> Digital infrastructure designed not merely to give communities sovereignty, but to teach them how to keep it.

## XIV. The Long Horizon
<!-- viz: generations | year: 88 | until: 100 | era: The Long Horizon | status: Handing On | accent: #f0c988 -->

The deepest test is not whether the first parish can install the system. It is whether **the hundredth steward** of that parish can inherit it.

The first generation will have founders who understand why the system exists. The second will inherit something it did not design. The third may no longer remember the technological world the project was reacting against.

A durable institution cannot depend on permanent revolutionary enthusiasm. Its practices must become **teachable, ordinary, comprehensible, and transmissible.**

Tradition is not the preservation of old things. It is **handing on:** receiving something one did not create, inhabiting it intelligently, caring for it faithfully, developing it prudently, and giving it to another generation.

>> The network should aim beyond decentralized infrastructure. It should establish a tradition of digital stewardship.

## Epilogue
<!-- viz: dawn | year: 100 | era: A Tradition | status: Handed On | accent: #f3d6a0 -->

If it succeeds, the decisive achievement will not be that families, parishes, schools, and communities possess their own servers.

It will be that people grow up knowing their digital world is not something distant corporations inevitably provide for them.

It is something they can understand. Something they can govern. Something they can care for together.

>>> And something they are responsible for handing on.
